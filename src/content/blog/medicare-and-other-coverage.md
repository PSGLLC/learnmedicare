Turning 65 doesn't always mean starting from zero. A lot of people already have some kind of health coverage in place — through a job, the VA, Medicaid, or TRICARE — and the first question is usually the same one: do I still need Medicare, and how do these two things work together? The honest answer is "it depends on which coverage you have," because each pairing follows different rules for enrollment timing, which plan pays first, and whether skipping Medicare now could cost you a penalty later. This guide walks through the four most common pairings so you can figure out where you stand before you make any decisions.

## Medicare and Employer Coverage

If you or your spouse is still working and you have group health coverage based on that current employment, ask the employer and Medicare which plan pays first. For many people age 65 or older, a current-employment group plan from an employer with **20 or more employees** pays first; with **fewer than 20 employees**, Medicare generally pays first. Special situations and multi-employer arrangements can change the result, so confirm your own payer order before delaying enrollment. See [Medicare's coordination rules](https://www.medicare.gov/health-drug-plans/coordination/who-pays-first).

If you delayed Part B because you or your spouse had group coverage based on **current employment**, you can generally sign up during an 8-month Part B Special Enrollment Period after employment ends or the group coverage ends, whichever happens first. If coverage ends while the person is still working, the 8-month period starts when coverage ends. COBRA or retiree coverage does not extend this Part B enrollment window. This Part B SEP does **not** depend on whether coverage is “creditable”; that term is used here for **prescription drug coverage under Part D**. Check the dates and your situation with [Medicare](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/sign-up/when-can-i-sign-up-for-medicare) and your benefits administrator. For a deeper look, see our guide on [working past 65](/working-at-65/).

## Medicare and the VA

VA health benefits do not replace Medicare, and Medicare and VA generally cannot pay for the same item or service. For each visit, you choose which benefit to use: Medicare pays for Medicare-covered services, while VA pays for VA-authorized services at a VA or non-VA facility. If VA authorizes care at a non-VA hospital but does not authorize every service, Medicare may cover eligible services VA did not authorize. Medicare coverage outside VA depends on the service and provider; do not assume a provider or visit is covered without checking. Read [Medicare's VA coordination guidance](https://www.medicare.gov/health-drug-plans/coordination/who-pays-first) and confirm authorization with VA.

## Medicare and Medicaid (Dual Eligibility)

Medicaid is a joint federal-state program with eligibility and benefits that vary by state; Medicare eligibility can be based on age or disability. People who qualify for both are called "dual eligible." For Medicare-covered services, Medicare pays first and Medicaid pays last after Medicare and other health insurance. Depending on the person's Medicaid eligibility category, the state may also help with Medicare premiums and cost sharing. See [Medicare's Medicaid guidance](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/costs/help/medicaid) and ask the state Medicaid office about individual eligibility and benefits.

This pairing can meaningfully lower out-of-pocket costs. The standard 2026 Part B premium was $202.90 per month with a $283 annual deductible. CMS announced the 2027 standard premium of $209.40 per month and annual deductible of $292 on November 17, 2026. For many dual-eligible beneficiaries, Medicaid or a related Medicare Savings Program helps cover some or all of these costs, subject to state program rules. If income is a concern as you approach 65, check eligibility with your state Medicaid office rather than assuming you do not qualify. See [CMS's current Medicare cost figures](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/2027-medicare-parts-b-premiums-deductibles). Our [Medicare vs. Medicaid](/medicare-vs-medicaid/) page breaks down the full comparison in more detail, including how the two programs differ in administration and covered services.

## Medicare and TRICARE for Life

Some TRICARE-eligible retirees, survivors, and family members qualify for TRICARE For Life (TFL) when they have Medicare Parts A and B. TFL coverage starts automatically when both parts are in effect, and the Part B premium is still due. In the United States, Medicare generally pays first and sends the claim to TRICARE; TRICARE pays for services it covers. When both programs cover a service, beneficiaries generally have no out-of-pocket cost, but coverage and payment order can differ when other insurance is involved or for care overseas. See [TRICARE's current TFL rules](https://www.tricare.mil/Plans/HealthPlans/TFL) and confirm your eligibility before delaying or dropping Part B.

## How to Decide What to Do With Other Coverage

Before deciding to enroll in Medicare, delay a part of it, or keep a secondary plan, a few questions are worth answering clearly:

- **Is my prescription drug coverage creditable for Part D?** This can affect a Part D late enrollment penalty. Ask your plan for its annual notice and see [Medicare's Part D creditable-coverage guidance](https://www.medicare.gov/health-drug-plans/part-d/basics/creditable-coverage). This is separate from the Part B Special Enrollment Period for current-employment coverage.
- **Which plan pays first?** Getting this backward can mean claims get denied or delayed, even when both plans would otherwise cover the service.
- **What's my Part B deadline if work coverage changes?** If you relied on group coverage based on current employment, the 8-month Part B SEP may start when employment or coverage ends, whichever comes first. A change in VA priority group or Medicaid eligibility is not the same Part B SEP; ask the relevant agency what enrollment rules apply to your situation.
- **Am I at risk of a penalty?** The [Medicare late enrollment penalties](/penalties/) for Part B and Part D follow different rules. Confirm the Part B employment-based SEP or whether drug coverage is creditable for Part D before delaying either part.

If any of this feels uncertain, it usually is — these rules have enough individual variation (employer size, state Medicaid program, TRICARE eligibility, or VA authorization) that a general guide can only get you so far. Checking directly with your plan or the relevant agency before an enrollment window closes is useful. Our [enrollment periods guide](/enrollment/) and [Medicare basics overview](/basics/) are good starting points if you want the full enrollment picture before working through how your specific situation fits in.

These coverage pairings follow different enrollment and payment rules. Before delaying Medicare or changing other coverage, confirm the rule for your situation with Medicare, your plan administrator, VA, Medicaid office, or TRICARE, and keep track of any applicable enrollment deadlines.
