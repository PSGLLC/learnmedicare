Every year, the Medicare Annual Enrollment Period runs October 15 through December 7, and every year, some people mean to make a change and simply run out of time. Life gets busy, a mailer gets set aside, or the decision feels too complicated to tackle before a hard deadline. If that's where you are right now, here's the good news first: missing AEP 2026 does not mean you lose your Medicare coverage. What it does mean is that your options narrow considerably, and it's worth understanding exactly what you're locked into — and what small exceptions still exist — before December 7 arrives.

This guide walks through what actually happens the day after AEP closes, who still has a narrow window to act, and how to avoid finding yourself in the same spot again next year.

## What Happens to Your Coverage on December 8

If you don't actively make a change during AEP 2026, nothing happens to your current coverage — and that's the key point. Whatever you're enrolled in on December 7 simply carries forward into Plan Year 2027, starting January 1. If you're on a Medicare Advantage plan, you stay on it. If you have Original Medicare with a standalone Part D plan, that continues too. You are not disenrolled, and you don't default to having no coverage at all.

The real cost of missing the deadline isn't a coverage gap — it's being stuck. If your current Medicare Advantage plan is raising costs, shrinking its network, or dropping a drug you need from its formulary for 2027, you generally can't do anything about it until the next enrollment opportunity rolls around. For a refresher on how AEP fits alongside Medicare's other enrollment windows, our [enrollment periods guide](/enrollment) breaks down each one side by side, including what happens if you miss them.

## The One Narrow Exception: Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment

If you're enrolled in a Medicare Advantage plan, there is one additional window after AEP closes: the Medicare Advantage Open Enrollment Period (OEP), which runs January 1 through March 31 every year. OEP lets you make one single change — switching to a different Medicare Advantage plan, or dropping Medicare Advantage altogether to return to Original Medicare (with the option to add a standalone Part D plan at that point).

OEP is genuinely useful if you discover early in the new year that your Medicare Advantage plan isn't working out, but it has real limits. You only get to use it once, you can't use it to switch from one standalone Part D plan to another, and it isn't available at all if you're on Original Medicare without Medicare Advantage. In other words, OEP is a safety net for Medicare Advantage members specifically — not a second AEP for everyone. Our [guide to comparing Medicare Advantage plans](/blog/compare-medicare-advantage-plans-aep/) can help you evaluate whether a plan is actually worth switching to if you do get to use this window.

## If You Have Original Medicare and a Standalone Part D Plan

This is the group with the least flexibility after December 7. If you're on Original Medicare with a standalone Part D prescription drug plan, missing AEP generally means you're locked into that same Part D plan for all of Plan Year 2027, with no equivalent to the Medicare Advantage OEP available to you. The only way around this is qualifying for a Special Enrollment Period (SEP) — for example, if you move out of your plan's service area, lose other creditable drug coverage, or experience certain other qualifying life events.

If none of those apply, your best move is simply to make sure the Part D plan you're already on still covers your medications reasonably well for 2027, even though you can't formally change plans. Our [guide to reviewing your Part D drug coverage](/blog/review-part-d-formulary-aep-2026/) walks through how to check a formulary, and the [Medicare costs page](/medicare-costs-2026) has the current Part D out-of-pocket cap figures, including the $2,100 annual out-of-pocket threshold for 2026, so you know what you're working with for the rest of this plan year.

## Avoiding a Repeat Next Year

If this year's deadline caught you off guard, a little structure now prevents the same scramble next October. A few things that help:

- **Mark AEP on next year's calendar early.** It runs the same dates every year — October 15 through December 7 — so there's no guessing involved.
- **Review your Annual Notice of Change (ANOC) as soon as it arrives**, usually by late September, rather than waiting until AEP is already underway. Our [guide to reading your ANOC](/blog/how-to-read-your-anoc/) explains what to look for.
- **Work with a licensed insurance agent before the window opens**, so comparisons and decisions are mostly made before the clock starts running, rather than during it.
- **Know your plan's star rating and network status going in**, so you're not starting research from zero once AEP begins. Our [Medicare Advantage Star Ratings guide](/blog/medicare-advantage-star-ratings/) explains what those ratings mean for your plan choice.

If you're newer to Medicare altogether and some of this terminology still feels unfamiliar, the [Medicare basics](/basics) page and our [glossary](/glossary) are good places to get oriented before the next enrollment window opens.

## What to Do Right Now If December 7 Is Still Ahead

If you're reading this before December 7 has actually passed, there's still time to act — AEP 2026 is open right now. Even a quick comparison of your current Medicare Advantage or Part D plan against what else is available in your area can be worth doing in the time remaining. You don't need to overhaul your coverage to benefit from AEP; sometimes the right move is confirming your current plan still fits, and other times it's catching a formulary or network change before it affects you in January.

Missing a deadline is stressful, but Medicare's rules are built so that a missed AEP window doesn't leave anyone without coverage — it just limits how soon you can fix something that isn't working. Understanding exactly where you stand, whether that's the OEP safety net, a possible Special Enrollment Period, or simply riding out the plan year, is the first step to making a good decision from here.
